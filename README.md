# Employee Attrition Analysis and Prediction

I analyzed employee attrition data and compared three classification models to understand patterns associated with employees leaving. This project includes my analysis notebook, visualizations, and written findings.

## Project files

- `analysis_1.ipynb` — my data exploration, preprocessing, model training, evaluation, visualizations, and HR recommendations.
- `WA_Fn-UseC_-HR-Employee-Attrition.csv` — the dataset I used in the analysis.
- `charts/` — five visualizations generated by the notebook.
- `summary_1.docx` — a written summary of my findings and recommendations.

## Dataset

I used the IBM HR Analytics employee attrition dataset. The CSV contains 1,470 records and 35 columns. The target column is `Attrition`, with 237 employees marked `Yes` (16.1%) and 1,233 marked `No` (83.9%).

I have not verified the original dataset publication or download URL.

## What I did

1. Inspected the dataset, its columns, and the attrition class distribution.
2. Checked for missing values, removed `EmployeeNumber`, `Over18`, `StandardHours`, and `EmployeeCount`, encoded the target and categorical variables, and scaled the features.
3. Explored attrition patterns by department, job role, monthly income, work-life balance, and tenure.
4. Trained and compared Logistic Regression, Random Forest, and Gradient Boosting classifiers.
5. Evaluated the models using precision, recall, F1-score, ROC-AUC, a classification report, and a confusion matrix.
6. Created five charts and developed HR recommendations based on the analysis.

I used an 80/20 train/test split with `random_state=42` and stratification by the target. I fit `StandardScaler` before splitting the data, which allowed information from the test set to influence preprocessing. This may make the reported test metrics optimistic. To reassess performance more reliably, I should split the data first and fit preprocessing only on the training set, ideally using a scikit-learn pipeline.

## Results

In the saved notebook results, Logistic Regression had the highest ROC-AUC and was selected as the best model:

| Model | Precision | Recall | F1-score | ROC-AUC |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Logistic Regression | 0.3412 | 0.6170 | 0.4394 | 0.7986 |
| Random Forest | 0.3750 | 0.0638 | 0.1091 | 0.7519 |
| Gradient Boosting | 0.5882 | 0.2128 | 0.3125 | 0.7941 |

These scores come from one train/test split and have not been independently reproduced. I consider them an initial model comparison, not a guarantee of performance on future employee data.

My exploratory analysis found an overall attrition rate of 16.1%, a rate of 20.6% in Sales, and a rate of 39.8% for Sales Representatives. Employees who left had an average monthly income 2,046 lower than employees who stayed. The notebook displays income with a rupee symbol, but I have not verified the dataset's currency, so I have not assigned a currency to that difference here.

## Limitations and responsible use

- The target classes are imbalanced: 16.1% of records are labeled as attrition.
- Scaling before the train/test split may have introduced data leakage into evaluation.
- The model results are based on one split and may not generalize to new data.
- The analysis includes demographic features. I recommend checking for fairness concerns before considering any HR use.
- A prediction indicates a pattern in historical data; it does not establish why an employee leaves or guarantee what an individual will do. I would use any risk estimate to support a conversation, not as the sole basis for an employment decision.
